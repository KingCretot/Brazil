Falando Pernambucano

The Northeast doesn't speak the Portuguese you hear in movies. Recife and the sertão have their own words, their own music, their own way of landing a joke. You don't need to be fluent — you need about eight of these. Use one correctly and you'll watch a face light up.

The essential eight

If you learn nothing else, learn these. Pronunciation is written the way an English speaker should say it — stressed syllable in CAPS.

Bom dia / Boa tarde / Boa noite
bohn JEE-ah · BOH-ah TAR-jee · BOH-ah NOY-chee
Good morning / afternoon / evening
Say it to everyone — entering a room, passing on the street, arriving at the site. Not saying it reads as cold.
Tudo bem? / Tudo bom?
TOO-doo BENG
All good? — the universal greeting
Answer is the same words back: "Tudo bem!" It's a greeting, not a real question.
Obrigado (men) / Obrigada (women)
oh-bree-GAH-doo / oh-bree-GAH-dah
Thank you
You match the ending to your own gender, not the other person's. This trips up every English speaker once.
Por favor
poor fah-VOR
Please
Com licença
kong lee-SEN-sah
Excuse me / pardon me
Use it to squeeze past someone, enter a home, or get attention politely.
Desculpa
jees-KOOL-pah
Sorry
Não entendi
nowng en-ten-JEE
I didn't understand
Follow with: "Mais devagar, por favor" (mice deh-vah-GAR) — "slower, please." You will use this daily.
Prazer!
prah-ZEHR
Pleasure — nice to meet you
Say it with the handshake or cheek kiss when meeting the families.

Pure Nordeste — the local flavor

This is the good stuff. These words mark you as someone who bothered to learn their Portuguese, not the Rio version. Use them lightly and with a smile — you're borrowing, not performing.

Visse?Recife's signature
VEE-see
Got it? / Right? / You know?
Tacked onto the end of sentences constantly in Recife. It's the single most Recife-identifying word there is. "Tá quente hoje, visse?"
Oxe! / Oxente!Top 3
OH-shee / oh-SHEN-chee
Whoa! / Huh?! / What on earth?
All-purpose expression of surprise, confusion, or mild outrage. Northeastern to the bone. Drop a well-timed "Oxe!" and people will laugh out loud.
Arretado / ArretadaTop 3
ah-heh-TAH-doo
Awesome, excellent, terrific
The Northeast's word for "great." A meal, a person, a day can all be arretado. Intensify it: "arretado da peste" = seriously fantastic.
Massa
MAH-sah
Cool / nice / awesome
Heavily used in Recife. Confusingly, massa also means the mortar you'll be mixing on site. Context sorts it out.
Eita!
AY-tah
Wow! / Yikes! / Oh boy!
Reaction to anything sudden. Bigger version: "Eita, gota serena!" — a classic Northeastern exclamation with no clean translation.
Vixe! / Vixe Maria!
VEE-shee
Oh my! / Oh no!
For bad news, near-misses, or dramatic sympathy. Pairs well with a hand on the chest.
Aperreado
ah-peh-hee-AH-doo
Stressed, bothered, hassled
Deeply Northeastern. "Tô aperreado" = I'm stressed out. Locals find it delightful when a foreigner uses this one right.
Bora!
BOH-rah
Let's go! / Come on!
Short for "vamos embora." Perfect for rallying the crew back to work after lunch. "Bora trabalhar!" = Let's get to work!
Danado / Danada
dah-NAH-doo
Clever, sharp — or mischievous
A compliment for a quick kid or a skilled worker. "Esse cabra é danado!"
Cabra da peste
KAH-brah dah PES-chee
A tough, admirable guy — a real character
Sounds like an insult, is actually high praise in the Northeast. A term of rugged respect.
Painho / Mainha
PIE-nyoo / MY-nyah
Dad / Mom
The Northeastern words for parents — you'll hear kids on site using them constantly.
Pense num calor!
PEN-see noong kah-LOR
"Imagine such heat!" — i.e., it's brutally hot
The "pense num..." construction is pure Nordeste and you can plug anything into it. You will have cause to use this one.
Água!
AH-gwah
Water!
Not slang — but in that heat it may be your most-used word. "Tô com sede" (toh kong SEH-jee) = I'm thirsty.

Food & the table

We'll be eating lunch in family homes. Complimenting the food is the fastest relationship-builder available to you — and Pernambuco's kitchen is worth complimenting.

Say this at the table

Está muito gostoso!
es-TAH MOO-ee-too gohs-TOH-zoo
This is delicious!
The single highest-value sentence in this entire document.
Está uma delícia!
es-TAH OO-mah deh-LEE-see-ah
It's a delight!
Quem cozinhou? Parabéns!
kayng koh-zee-NYOH · pah-rah-BENGZ
Who cooked? Congratulations!
Watch what this does to a room.
Estou satisfeito / satisfeita.
es-TOH sah-chees-FAY-too
I'm full (satisfied).
The polite way to stop the third helping. Saying "estou cheio" (I'm stuffed) is blunter.

soh oong poh-KEE-nyoo

soh veh-zheh-tah-ree-AH-noo

Culture & cautions

⚠️ The one word you must never use: rapariga.
In Portugal and in your phrasebook app, rapariga simply means "girl." In the Northeast of Brazil it means prostitute. It is a serious insult. Use menina (meh-NEE-nah) for a girl, moça (MOH-sah) for a young woman, mulher (moo-LYEHR) for a woman. Tell every member of the team.
A few more to handle with care. Abestado (dopey/foolish) and mangar (to mock) are affectionate between friends but sting from a stranger — understand them, don't deploy them. And skip égua — that's Belém, not Recife, and using it here marks you as someone who learned Brazil from the internet.

How people greet

  • Cheek kisses. Women greet women and men with a light cheek-touch kiss — typically two in Recife, right cheek first. Men greet men with a handshake, often with a shoulder clasp or a hug if there's warmth. Follow the other person's lead; nobody will be offended if you hesitate.
  • Physical closeness is normal. Standing near, touching an arm while talking, extended eye contact — this is friendliness, not intrusion. Backing away reads as coldness.
  • Greet the room, not the group. Walking into a home or a work site, people greet each person individually. It takes an extra minute and it matters enormously.
  • The OK hand sign (thumb + forefinger circle) is obscene in Brazil. Use a thumbs-up instead — that one's universal and beloved here.
  • "Sim" is rarely used alone. To say yes, Brazilians usually repeat the verb: "Você gostou?" → "Gostei!" Don't worry about mastering this; just know that a plain "sim" sounds a bit clipped.

The music you'll hear — know the names

  • Frevo (FREH-voo) — Recife's frantic, brass-driven carnival music and its acrobatic umbrella dance. The city's pride.
  • Maracatu (mah-rah-kah-TOO) — thunderous Afro-Brazilian drum processions with royal courts and costumes. This is what you'll hear on Black Consciousness Day.
  • Forró (foh-HOH) — accordion, triangle, zabumba drum; the dance music of the interior. If someone pulls you up to dance, say yes. You'll be bad at it. That's the point.
  • Ciranda and coco — circle dances of the coast and countryside. Anyone can join a ciranda; you just hold hands and step.

Three things that will carry you further than vocabulary

  • Try, badly, out loud. Northeasterners are famously warm toward people who make the attempt. Perfect silence reads as aloofness; a botched "Oxe!" reads as affection.
  • Learn and use names. Ask "Como é seu nome?" (KOH-moo eh seh-oo NOH-mee) and then actually use it. On a build site, being called by name is being seen.
  • Let them feed you. Refusing food or drink in a home is a bigger deal than any grammatical error you could possibly make.
